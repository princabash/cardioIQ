What “optimal” actually means — and where the targets come from

“Normal” on a lab report compares you to the population. Optimal asks a harder question: what level keeps your arteries on the lowest-risk path for the decades ahead — given your risk? That target isn't something we invent. It rests on three well-established ideas:

  • Targets scale with risk. Guidelines (ESC/EAS) set cholesterol targets by risk category, and they get stricter as risk rises. Your optimal isn't everyone's optimal — it's calibrated to you.
  • Particle count and inherited risk matter. ApoB (the atherogenic particle count) and Lp(a) (inherited) capture risk a standard panel can miss entirely.
  • Cumulative exposure drives disease. Lower, earlier, and for longer means less plaque over a lifetime — so “optimal” is a long-horizon target, not just a snapshot.

How your report is built

Five steps, and no black box at any of them:

  1. Your numbers, organized. Your panel and risk factors are structured into a consistent profile.
  2. Mapped to risk. Each marker is linked to what it means for cardiovascular risk, using a physician-built clinical knowledge graph.
  3. Anchored to the evidence. Those relationships are checked against current guidelines and named trials — never vague “studies show.”
  4. Written in a physician's reasoning. Each read follows the same chain a clinician uses: the finding → what's optimal for you → why it matters → what changes it → what to discuss → where interpretation ends and your doctor begins.
  5. Kept current. As guidelines and major trials update, the underlying evidence base updates — and your interpretation reflects it.

What we anchor to

We cite sources, not vibes. The evidence base includes:

  • ESC/EAS dyslipidaemia guidelines (2019, with the 2025 Focused Update) — LDL / ApoB targets by risk category, with risk assessed via SCORE2/SCORE2-OP
  • EAS consensus statements — Lp(a), and ApoB as a preferred marker
  • ACC/AHA primary-prevention guidance and the PREVENT risk equations
  • ADA and KDIGO standards where cardiometabolic and kidney risk overlap
  • Named cardiovascular outcomes trials, cited directly

If a position changes in the guidelines, it changes here.

What CardioIQ is — and isn't

Being clear about the limits is part of the method. Plainly:

CardioIQ is

  • Physician-built and guideline-anchored
  • Calibrated to longevity-optimal targets and your risk
  • A clear, structured interpretation you can act on and take to your doctor
  • Kept current as the evidence moves

CardioIQ is not

  • A diagnosis or a medical device
  • A treatment plan or a promise of any outcome
  • A replacement for your physician, who makes every clinical decision
  • A regulatory-cleared clinical service

A tool that's honest about what a report can't do is one you can trust with what it does.
