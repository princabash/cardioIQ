When "Normal" Isn't Optimal: Insulin Resistance and Your Heart

Reviewed by Tea Gamezardashvili, MD, PhD, MHA, FACC
Preventive Cardiologist & Lipidologist · President, Georgian Atherosclerosis Association

Most people meet insulin resistance years before any test calls it "prediabetes" — and often while every number on the page still reads normal. That gap between normal and optimal is where a lot of cardiovascular risk quietly accumulates. The arteries you'll have at 70 are largely built by the biology and choices of your 40s. This is a story about reading the early signals, not the late ones.

What insulin resistance actually is

Insulin is the hormone that lets your cells take up glucose from the blood after you eat. When cells stop responding well to it — the "lock" no longer turns easily with the same key — your body compensates by making more insulin to get the same job done.

Here's the part that matters for prevention: for a long time, this compensation works. Your blood glucose can stay in the normal range for years while your insulin levels climb behind the scenes. By the time fasting glucose or HbA1c drifts high enough to trigger a "prediabetes" label, the underlying process has usually been running for a while. Insulin resistance is an early chapter; a glucose reading is often a late one.

Why your heart notices before your glucose does

Insulin resistance doesn't just affect sugar — it reshapes your lipids. As it develops, the blood tends to carry more triglyceride-rich particles, HDL ("good" cholesterol) tends to fall, and LDL particles tend to become smaller and denser. Cardiologists call this pattern atherogenic dyslipidemia, and it's one of the reasons metabolic health and heart health can't really be separated.

The American Diabetes Association draws its lines for prediabetes at a fasting glucose of 100–125 mg/dL or an HbA1c of 5.7–6.4%. Those thresholds are useful — but risk is a gradient, not a switch that flips at 100. Someone sitting at the high end of "normal," with rising insulin and a shifting lipid pattern, can already be on a trajectory worth understanding early.

The number that hides the risk

Here's the trap: in insulin resistance, your LDL-C (the standard cholesterol number) can look perfectly "normal" while the actual number of harmful particles in your blood is high. That's because LDL-C measures the cholesterol carried, not the count of particles. When particles are small and dense — as they often are in metabolic dysfunction — you can have many particles carrying a normal-looking cholesterol total.

This is where ApoB comes in

ApoB counts the atherogenic particles directly — every LDL and related particle carries exactly one ApoB molecule, so one blood test gives you the particle burden your arteries actually see. When ApoB and LDL-C disagree — a situation called discordance — it's ApoB that tracks the risk more faithfully. And discordance is especially common in people with elevated triglycerides, insulin resistance, or metabolic syndrome. That's exactly why the ESC/EAS dyslipidaemia guidelines highlight ApoB as the preferred measure in these situations. (If ApoB is new to you, start with our complete guide to ApoB.)

Put simply: if you have any sign of insulin resistance, a "normal" LDL-C alone may be reassuring you too soon. ApoB is how you check. (There's also Lp(a) — an inherited, independent risk factor worth measuring once in a lifetime — which sits alongside all of this. If you're already looking at your lipids, it's worth knowing that number too. We cover the difference in Lp(a) vs LDL-C and the target ranges in ApoB & Lp(a) targets.)

Two markers deserve a note. Fasting insulin (and the HOMA-IR index calculated from it) often moves early, while glucose still looks fine — which makes it one of the more forward-looking numbers here (more on that in fasting insulin and longevity). And the triglyceride-to-HDL relationship is a rough surrogate clinicians sometimes use to flag insulin resistance and small dense LDL. Neither is a diagnosis on its own; both are context.

How a clinician reads one value

A single number means little without its frame. Take ApoB 112 mg/dL: on its own, unremarkable. But read against a longevity-optimal target rather than the lab's population range, alongside a low HDL, a rising fasting insulin, and a family history — it reads very differently. The value is the start of the interpretation, never the end of it. That interpretation belongs with your physician, who sees your whole picture.

What you can actually do about it

The encouraging part: insulin resistance is one of the most modifiable chapters in cardiovascular risk, and lifestyle is genuinely first-line — not a consolation prize offered instead of "real" treatment. Nutrition, physical activity, and sleep all act as direct levers on these biomarkers, and they're always presented alongside, not below, medical options.

  • Movement is insulin medicine. Muscle contraction pulls glucose out of the blood in a way that doesn't fully depend on insulin — which is why regular activity, including simple resistance work, improves insulin sensitivity.
  • The plate shapes the pattern. Reducing refined carbohydrate and added sugar tends to lower triglycerides and ease the atherogenic lipid pattern that rides along with insulin resistance.
  • Sleep is a metabolic variable. Short or poor sleep worsens insulin sensitivity — it belongs in the same conversation as diet and exercise, not in a separate one.
  • Medication is a physician's decision. When lifestyle alone isn't enough, there are well-established medication classes your doctor may consider. The specific choice is theirs to make with you — this article names none as "what you should take."

Underneath all of it is a single principle from the science of prevention: risk is driven by cumulative exposure — how high, for how long, starting how early. Genetic studies point the same way: lower particle burden, maintained for longer and begun sooner, is what changes the long arc. That's why acting on an early signal in your 40s is worth so much more than reacting to a diagnosis in your 60s.

The prevention loop

Every marker here connects to the same measurable cycle. It's not a one-time reading — it's a loop you and your physician run over years.
