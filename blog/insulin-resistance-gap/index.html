Your glucose is "normal."
So why are your arteries aging?

Fasting glucose and HbA1c can read perfectly normal for years while insulin resistance quietly does its work. This is the insulin resistance gap — and it may be one of the most under-measured drivers of cardiovascular aging.

Metabolic health Reviewed against CardioIQ's clinical knowledge base · 9 min read

You get your annual labs back. Fasting glucose is 88 mg/dL — textbook normal. HbA1c is 5.4%, well under the diabetes line. On paper, your metabolism looks fine.

And yet: energy dips after meals, weight that settles around the midsection, triglycerides that keep creeping up. These can be early signals of something a routine screen is not built to catch — insulin resistance that has been present, and quietly influencing your arteries, long before glucose ever moves.

This article explains why that gap exists, which two numbers close it, and what a reasonable next step looks like — always in partnership with your own physician.

Glucose is a lagging indicator

Standard metabolic screening leans on two tests: fasting glucose (reference: below 100 mg/dL) and HbA1c (reference: below 5.7%). Both are genuinely good at one job — confirming diabetes once it has developed. They are far less useful for detecting the earlier stage, when the trajectory is still very changeable.

Why the numbers stay quiet

When cells become less responsive to insulin — driven by genetics, visceral fat, low activity, poor sleep, and diet quality — the pancreas does not fail straight away. It compensates by producing more insulin to move the same glucose into cells. For a long stretch, sometimes a decade or more, this compensation works: insulin rises, glucose is pushed to stay in range, and both fasting glucose and HbA1c continue to read "normal."

Meanwhile, chronically elevated insulin is associated with visceral fat accumulation (including in the liver), higher triglycerides and lower HDL, impaired blood-vessel function, and a more inflammatory internal environment. By the time glucose finally drifts into the prediabetes range, that quieter phase has often been underway for years.

The gap in one line: a normal glucose tells you the pancreas is still winning the fight. It does not tell you how hard it is fighting — or what that effort is costing your arteries.

The two numbers that close the gap

The missing measurement is fasting insulin, and the calculation built from it, HOMA-IR. Together they show how much insulin your body needs to keep glucose where it is — which is exactly the information a glucose value leaves out.

This is also where CardioIQ's core idea applies: the gap between a population-normal range and a longevity-optimal one. A lab may flag a value only when it is frankly abnormal; the more useful question for prevention is where you sit relative to optimal.

HOMA-IR: putting glucose and insulin together

HOMA-IR (Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance) combines fasting insulin and fasting glucose into a single estimate of insulin sensitivity.

The calculation

HOMA-IR = ( fasting insulin [mU/L] × fasting glucose [mmol/L] ) ÷ 22.5

To convert glucose from mg/dL to mmol/L, divide by 18.
Example: glucose 90 mg/dL (5.0 mmol/L), insulin 15 mU/L → (15 × 5.0) ÷ 22.5 = 3.3. That points to meaningful insulin resistance — even though the glucose reading alone looked reassuring.

Reference points only; thresholds differ across populations and assays. A number is a prompt for a conversation, not a verdict. If you want to go deeper on the metabolic side, our companion piece on fasting insulin and longevity looks more closely at these markers.

How insulin resistance ages arteries

The reason this matters for the heart is that insulin resistance influences several of the pathways that drive atherosclerosis — and it does so largely independently of glucose. A few of the better-understood links:

It reshapes your lipids toward the atherogenic pattern

Insulin resistance tends to raise triglycerides, lower HDL, and shift LDL toward smaller, denser particles. Crucially, it is a leading reason that LDL-C and ApoB diverge. ApoB counts the actual number of atherogenic particles, and the EAS consensus positions it as the preferred marker of that particle burden — superior to LDL-C precisely when triglycerides are high or metabolic syndrome is present. UK Biobank and CPRD analyses show these two markers disagree in a meaningful minority of people — on the order of one in four or five. In that group, an LDL-C that looks fine can sit alongside an ApoB that does not.

This is why insulin resistance and atherogenic particle burden are best read together, not separately. If you want the fuller picture on particle count, start with our ApoB pillar.

It promotes inflammation and vascular strain

Insulin-resistant fat tissue releases inflammatory signals that raise markers such as hsCRP — the same inflammatory residual risk highlighted by the JUPITER trial. Alongside this, impaired blood-vessel dilation and rising blood pressure add to the load on the arterial wall. None of these show up on a glucose test.

Who might reasonably consider these tests

Fasting insulin and HOMA-IR are worth raising with your physician if you have an otherwise "normal" glucose alongside any of the following. None of these is a diagnosis on its own — they simply raise the value of looking closer:

  • Increasing waist circumference, or weight that concentrates around the midsection
  • An atherogenic lipid pattern: triglycerides above ~150 mg/dL, low HDL, a high TG/HDL ratio, or an elevated ApoB / non-HDL-C
  • Blood pressure that is drifting upward year over year
  • A family history of type 2 diabetes or premature cardiovascular disease
  • Related conditions such as fatty liver (raised ALT), PCOS, or sleep apnea
  • Simply being 40+ and wanting an honest metabolic baseline

What you can actually do about it

The encouraging part: early insulin resistance is one of the more modifiable risk factors in cardiovascular prevention. Lifestyle change is genuinely first-line here, and for many people it moves these numbers meaningfully.

Lifestyle — the foundation

  • Nutrition: fewer refined carbohydrates and sugar-sweetened drinks; more fiber, whole foods, adequate protein, and unsaturated fats. Giving the body longer gaps between meals may help some people, though the right pattern is individual.
  • Movement: a mix of resistance training and regular aerobic activity improves how the body handles glucose. Even a short walk after meals helps, and a single session can improve insulin sensitivity for a day or two.
  • Weight, if relevant: for those carrying excess visceral fat, a modest reduction (around 5–10% of body weight) can improve insulin sensitivity substantially.
  • Sleep and stress: short or poor-quality sleep and chronic stress both worsen insulin resistance. Protecting 7–9 hours and treating sleep apnea matter more than most people expect.

When lifestyle isn't enough

If focused lifestyle change doesn't move the numbers, several classes of medication can improve insulin sensitivity or support weight loss and metabolic health. Which class — if any — is appropriate depends on your full risk picture, and that is a decision for you and your physician together. CardioIQ does not recommend specific drugs; that judgment belongs in the clinic.

The same applies to supplements often discussed in this space. Evidence is mixed and dosing is individual, so treat them as something to raise with your physician rather than to self-prescribe.

Why this fits a longevity view of the heart

Cardiovascular disease doesn't begin on the day of a diagnosis. Under a cumulative-exposure model — lower risk factors, held for longer, starting earlier — the quiet insulin-resistant years are precisely the window where intervention changes the long-term trajectory. Waiting for glucose to become abnormal is, in effect, waiting for a later and harder starting point.

Two accessible numbers — fasting insulin and HOMA-IR — turn that silent phase into something you can see, discuss, and act on while the trajectory is still very much yours to bend.
