Understanding Your Results

How to Read Your Lipid Panel — and What "Normal" Misses

Reviewed by Tea Gamezardashvili, MD, PhD, MHA, FACC
Preventive Cardiologist & Lipidologist · President, Georgian Atherosclerosis Association

You have your cholesterol results in front of you. There's a column of numbers, maybe a few flagged "high" or "normal," and not much explanation. This is a plain-language walk through every line — what each one means, what the "normal" ranges actually represent, and the two numbers a standard panel usually leaves out entirely. The goal isn't to alarm you. It's to help you read your own results with the same lens a preventive cardiologist would.

What's actually on a standard lipid panel

A basic cholesterol test — the one most doctors order — typically reports four or five numbers. Here's what each one is measuring, alongside the reference ranges most labs print (US units, mg/dL). Read these as population reference ranges, not personal targets — the difference matters, and we'll come back to it.

<100 optimal · 130–159 borderline · ≥160 high
HDL-C
Cholesterol carried by HDL ("good") particles
<40 low (a risk factor) · ≥60 protective
Triglycerides
A fat in the blood; rises with refined carbs, alcohol, insulin resistance
<150 normal · 150–199 borderline · ≥200 high
Non-HDL cholesterol
Total minus HDL — all the "atherogenic" cholesterol at once
Roughly your LDL goal + 30

Ranges follow long-standing US (ATP III) conventions and can differ by lab, units (mg/dL vs mmol/L), and country. Your physician sets the target that applies to you.

If your panel shows non-HDL cholesterol, pay attention to it — it's quietly one of the more useful lines. It captures the cholesterol in every harmful particle (LDL plus triglyceride-rich "remnants"), which makes it more reliable than LDL-C alone when triglycerides are up. It's the first hint of the deeper idea in this article: what matters isn't just how much cholesterol you have, but how many particles are carrying it.

Why a "normal" LDL-C can still mislead

Here's the mechanism that surprises most people. LDL-C measures the cholesterol cargo, not the number of trucks carrying it. Two people can have the exact same LDL-C — say, a tidy 100 mg/dL — while one is carrying that cholesterol in a few large particles and the other in many small, dense ones. It's the particle count that keeps making contact with your artery walls, so the second person can carry meaningfully more risk with an identical-looking number.

This mismatch has a name — discordance — and it's most common in exactly the people who most want a clear answer: those with higher triglycerides, insulin resistance, or metabolic syndrome. In those cases, an LDL-C that reads "normal" can sit alongside a particle burden that doesn't. Your standard panel simply can't see the difference.

The one-sentence version

A standard lipid panel tells you how much cholesterol you're carrying. It doesn't tell you how many particles are carrying it, or whether you inherited an extra risk that isn't measured at all. Two of the most important numbers usually aren't on the page.

The two numbers your panel probably left out

These aren't exotic or experimental. They're standard tools in preventive cardiology — they're just not part of the basic panel most people receive.

ApoBparticle count
Every atherogenic particle carries exactly one ApoB molecule, so this single number counts the trucks directly. When ApoB and LDL-C disagree, ApoB tracks the risk more faithfully — which is why the ESC/EAS guidelines highlight it, especially when triglycerides are high or metabolic syndrome is present. Lp(a)inherited risk
Lp(a) is a genetically determined particle that raises cardiovascular risk independently of everything else on your panel — and it's essentially never included in a standard test. It's largely fixed for life, so it only needs to be measured once. Read the full Lp(a) guide →

If you've only ever had a basic panel, there's a reasonable chance neither of these has been checked. That's not a failing on anyone's part — it reflects what a standard order includes. It's simply worth knowing they exist, and worth asking about. (If you want to see how the "count" view differs from the "cargo" view, our guide on what a normal ApoB looks like is a good next read.)

"Normal" versus optimal

This is the distinction that changes how you read every line above. A lab flags a value "normal" when it falls inside the range of a broad population — a population that includes a lot of people who are not aging their arteries slowly. "Normal" means "common." It does not mean "ideal for a long, healthy life."

Preventive and longevity-focused cardiology works from risk-based, optimal targets instead. The clearest example: modern ESC/EAS guidelines set LDL-C goals well below the familiar "normal" line for people at higher risk — for the highest-risk group, a goal under 55 mg/dL, far below the <100 that reads "optimal" on most lab printouts. The right target for you depends on your overall risk, which is precisely the judgment your physician makes with your full picture. The point isn't a magic number — it's that "in range" and "as good as it should be" are two different questions.

Underneath it sits one principle from the science of prevention: risk is driven by cumulative exposure — how high your particle burden is, for how long, starting how early. That's why reading your results in your 40s against an optimal target, rather than waiting for a value to cross into "high," is worth so much.

Reading one panel — an illustration

A composite example, assembled to show how the numbers fit together — not a real patient, and not a diagnosis. Someone gets back: total cholesterol 195, LDL-C 98, HDL-C 41, triglycerides 190. Every line reads "normal" or close to it, and they're told they're fine. But the low-ish HDL and elevated triglycerides hint at an atherogenic, insulin-resistance-leaning pattern — the setting where LDL-C most often under-states particle burden. The natural next step isn't worry; it's to add an ApoB (and a one-time Lp(a)) and read the whole thing against optimal. The value is in the pattern, not any single line.

What to do with your results

You don't need to become your own cardiologist. You need to know which questions your numbers raise, and then bring them to someone who can answer them for your specific situation.

  • Note the pattern, not just the flags. Low HDL with high triglycerides, or a "normal" LDL-C in someone with metabolic risk, is worth a closer look even when nothing is flagged.
  • Ask about the two missing numbers. "Can we add an ApoB to my next panel?" and "Have I ever had my Lp(a) measured?" are reasonable questions to bring to your doctor.
  • Read against optimal, not just normal. Ask what target fits your risk — not just whether you're inside the population range.
  • Then repeat. Lipids respond to changes over months, so the real value is in the loop below, run over years with your physician.
